Artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, and generative AI—the things people often mix up. Already sounds massive, right? But hold on. It’s easier than you think.
They’re all connected. But to understand the difference, let’s follow one patient through a hospital.
First, artificial intelligence is the broad field. It includes systems that recognize patterns, interpret language, or apply knowledge to solve problems.
Some AI systems work with knowledge people put into them.
Imagine a clinical expert system. Medical experts supply rules, and the system applies those rules to our patient’s symptoms and findings to suggest possibilities.
The system can use medical knowledge without learning it from patient records.
But what if the patterns we need are too complicated to write down as rules?
Suppose we want to estimate whether our patient will return to hospital after discharge.
We have thousands of previous admissions: diagnoses, lab results, patient histories—and we know which patients came back.
Instead of telling the computer exactly how those details should affect the prediction, we use them to train a model.
It makes predictions, compares them with the known outcomes, and adjusts its internal settings to reduce the errors.
That is machine learning: learning patterns from examples.
Once trained, the model can apply those patterns to a new patient. Using a trained model is called inference.
Training is where it learns from the examples. Inference is where we use what it learned.
Now our patient has a chest X-ray.
The information is no longer neatly arranged in columns. It’s inside an image: millions of pixels, with subtle patterns that may matter.
Deep learning is a type of machine learning that uses neural networks with many layers.
During training, those layers learn useful representations of the image—from simpler visual features to more complex patterns that help identify a finding.
We don’t have to describe every relevant shape by hand. The network learns features from the training examples.
That’s why deep learning became so useful for images. It also works with sound, language, and other kinds of data.
Now our patient is going home, and we want a draft discharge summary.
This time, we’re asking the system to create something.
That brings us to generative AI.
A model trained on large amounts of text learns patterns in language. Given the patient’s documented information and an instruction, it can generate a draft summary.
Large language models are one example. Other generative models create images, audio, or video.
Many of these systems start with a foundation model: a model trained on a vast amount of data that can be adapted to different tasks.
Instead of building a completely separate model for every use, we can start with that foundation.
So here’s how the four terms connect.
Artificial intelligence is the broad field.
Machine learning is an approach within AI that learns patterns from data.
Deep learning is a type of machine learning built with many-layered neural networks.
Generative AI describes systems that generate content. Many modern generative systems are built using deep learning.
These terms overlap. They tell us different things about the same technology.
And why did its capabilities grow so dramatically?
Three things came together: much more data, better training algorithms, and the computing power to use them.
GPUs are a major part of that story. They can perform many calculations in parallel—the kind of work neural networks need during training and use.
That’s the connection between a chip company like NVIDIA and the AI tools arriving in medicine.
For our patient, we’ve gone from applying medical knowledge, to predicting a risk, to analyzing an image, to drafting a document.
But every step still needs to be tested for the task it performs.
Learning from patient records doesn’t automatically mean working well on new patients. And generating a discharge summary doesn’t guarantee that every sentence matches the record.
Understanding AI starts here: knowing what the system learned, what we’re asking it to do, and whether there’s evidence it can do that job.
Because “AI” is a very broad name.
Our patients need us to be more specific.
