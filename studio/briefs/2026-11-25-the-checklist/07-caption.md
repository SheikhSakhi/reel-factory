In 1935, the most advanced airplane in the world crashed on its first big test flight. Nothing was broken. The controls were still locked: one forgotten step, among too many to remember.

Pilots didn't try to fly better. They wrote the steps down. The checklist was born, and that plane became the B-17.

Medicine learned it once: a one-page surgical safety checklist across eight hospitals was followed by a fall in deaths after surgery from 1.5% to 0.8% (Haynes et al., NEJM 2009).

Now imagine that for the months between visits: a written plan for each patient. What to check. When to act. Who to call.

The best pilot didn't need more skill. He needed a list. So does the best doctor.
